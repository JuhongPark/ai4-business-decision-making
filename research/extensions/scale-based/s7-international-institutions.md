# Tier S7 — International Institutions

status: research_in_progress
tier: S7
date: 2026-04-13

## 1. Tier Definition and Scope

Tier S7 covers cross-border standards bodies and intergovernmental organizations whose outputs are addressed to sovereign states rather than to firms or individuals. The reference set used here is the United Nations Secretariat and its specialized agencies (UN, UNCTAD, WHO, ITU, ICAO, FAO), the OECD, the World Bank Group, the IMF, the WTO, and bodies created specifically to govern AI such as the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the GPAI working groups (now hosted under the OECD). At this tier, "verification" is not a technical control over a single AI output. It is the question of whether an AI-generated assessment can survive an inter-state legitimacy test: peer review across delegations, multilingual translation, intergovernmental clearance, and incorporation into a non-binding recommendation, a binding instrument, or a country-level surveillance report. The verification problem is therefore *legitimacy across sovereign jurisdictions*, not factual correctness alone.

## 2. Core Question Answered

When an international body adopts an AI-generated assessment, the verification discipline that is actually applied before that assessment enters a resolution, communique, or binding instrument is overwhelmingly *committee-mediated and document-genealogy-based*, not algorithmic or AI-specific. Across the documented cases examined here (OECD AI Capability Indicators, UK-led International AI Safety Report, OECD AI Incidents Monitor, IMF Article IV staff reports, IPCC review process used as the design template for the new UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI), verification proceeds through (a) drafting by a secretariat or staff team, (b) iterated expert review, (c) interdepartmental clearance, (d) member-state delegate review through standing working parties, and (e) ministerial or executive-board adoption. The same chain is being explicitly imported by the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI from the IPCC model [B] [Independent International Scientific Panel on AI](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en).

The chain delivers high *legitimacy* but only patchy *reasoning verification* in the RVCS-1 sense. Source quality and normative fit are reviewed seriously because every member state has standing to object on those grounds. Inferential validity is reviewed informally through expert reviewer comments. Confidence calibration is treated heterogeneously: the International AI Safety Report explicitly catalogues areas of expert disagreement, but most OECD and IMF outputs use hedged language without a base-rate reference [A] [International AI Safety Report 2025](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2025). Failure mode awareness is the weakest link: there is no standardized AI-failure catalog that a working party is required to consult before adopting an AI-derived input, and the explicit hardware-and-compute verification mechanisms proposed by Oxford AIGI in 2025 are scoped to *inter-state compliance*, not to the verification of AI-generated reasoning inside the institution itself [A] [Verification for International AI Governance, Harack et al. 2025](https://aigi.ox.ac.uk/publications/verification-for-international-ai-governance/).

The practical answer to the core question is therefore: international institutions verify the *political legitimacy* of an AI-derived claim with high discipline, but they verify the *epistemic structure* of that claim only weakly, through generic expert review processes designed before generative AI existed. The gap is acknowledged in the OECD's beta status of the AI Capability Indicators and in the design of the UN Scientific Panel as an "evidence engine" intended to fill exactly this hole [B] [Constructing a framework to measure AI capabilities, OECD 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/introducing-the-oecd-ai-capability-indicators_be745f04-en/full-report/component-5.html).

## 3. Comparative Dimensions

### D1 — Verification agent identity

The verifier is a layered set of standing committees and working parties, not an individual. For OECD AI outputs, the Working Party on AI Governance (AIGO) under the Digital Policy Committee is the primary standing reviewer [A] [OECD Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/oecd-legal-0449). For IMF surveillance, the verifier is a chain of staff team, area department, peer review department, and management clearance before the Executive Board [A] [IMF Guidance Note for Surveillance under Article IV](https://www.imf.org/external/np/pp/eng/2015/031915.pdf). For the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, the verifier set is 40 panel members representing five UN regions selected from over 2,600 applicants, plus working groups [B] [Independent International Scientific Panel on AI press release](https://press.un.org/en/2026/sgsm23034.doc.htm). The IPCC model assigns named Review Editors per chapter whose explicit job is to make sure all expert and government comments are addressed [A] [IPCC Factsheet: How does the IPCC review process work?](https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/2018/02/FS_review_process.pdf).

### D2 — Verification infrastructure

Standing committees (OECD AIGO, IMF Executive Board, IPCC Bureau, ITU/ISO/IEC joint groups), online expert-comment portals (IPCC), document genealogies of drafts and review responses, the OECD AI Incidents Monitor as a structured reporting instrument with 29 criteria across 8 dimensions [A] [Towards a Common Reporting Framework for AI Incidents, OECD 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/towards-a-common-reporting-framework-for-ai-incidents_f326d4ac-en.html), the AI Standards Exchange Database with 870+ AI standards under ITU/ISO/IEC [B] [International AI Standards Exchange](https://aiforgood.itu.int/ai-standards-exchange/). The Oxford AIGI 2025 verification report adds a *future* infrastructure proposal — narrow-purpose verification hardware in data centers and mutually verified data centers — but those address inter-state compliance, not internal reasoning review [A] [Verification for International AI Governance](https://aigi.ox.ac.uk/publications/verification-for-international-ai-governance/).

### D3 — Verification incentive structure

Three incentives stack at this tier. (i) *Diplomatic legitimacy*: a recommendation that any single member state can credibly reject as ill-reasoned will not be adopted, so the secretariat has strong incentive to anticipate every plausible objection [B] inferred from OECD adoption process. (ii) *Comparative reputation* against rival bodies: OECD, UN, and ITU are in implicit competition to be the authoritative AI standards venue, and an embarrassing factual or reasoning error degrades that position [B] [CSIS analysis of UN Global Dialogue power shifts](https://www.csis.org/analysis/what-un-global-dialogue-ai-governance-reveals-about-global-power-shifts). (iii) *Scientific independence*: bodies that publish under named chairs (e.g., Bengio for the International AI Safety Report) accept some personal reputational risk that increases the rigor of expert review [A] [Wikipedia: International AI Safety Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_AI_Safety_Report).

### D4 — Sanction gradient

Sanctions for verification failure at S7 are diffuse but politically severe. There are essentially no legal liabilities — the OECD AI Principles are explicitly non-binding [A] [OECD AI Principles Overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles), and IMF Article IV reports are advisory not enforceable. The dominant sanction is *legitimacy loss*: a state withdrawing endorsement (the United States declined to back the 2026 International AI Safety Report after backing the 2025 edition) [A] [Time: U.S. Withholds Support From Global AI Safety Report](https://time.com/7364551/ai-impact-summit-safety-report/), public retraction of a working paper, or having a competing institution publish a contradictory analysis. Reputational sanctions can be partially monetized (donor funding, secondment of national experts, hosting of summits), but the gradient is shallow compared to firm-level civil liability at S4 or to professional sanctions at S5.

### D5 — Verification trigger

Triggers are calendar-driven and ratification-driven, not threshold-driven. Every Article IV consultation triggers staff-report verification on a roughly annual cycle [A] [IMF Policy Advice factsheet](https://www.imf.org/en/About/Factsheets/IMF-Surveillance). OECD principle revisions trigger verification at Ministerial Council Meetings on multi-year cycles (2019 adoption, 2024 update) [A] [OECD Updates AI Principles, ANSI 2024](https://www.ansi.org/standards-news/all-news/5-9-24-oecd-updates-ai-principles). IPCC review cycles run on six-to-seven-year assessment-report rhythms. The new UN Scientific Panel is mandated to deliver an annual report [B] [UN news on UN AI governance bodies](https://news.un.org/en/story/2025/09/1165898). This calendar discipline is the strongest verification mechanism at S7 — it forces re-examination on a known schedule, but it also delays re-examination of a flawed claim until the next cycle.

### D6 — Per-decision verification budget

For a flagship deliverable, the budget is enormous in absolute terms but tiny per claim. The OECD AI Capability Indicators were developed over five years by 50+ experts, with chapters written by 32 experts and reviewed by 25 [A] [Constructing a framework to measure AI capabilities](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/introducing-the-oecd-ai-capability-indicators_be745f04-en/full-report/component-5.html). The International AI Safety Report 2025 mobilized 100 experts from 30+ countries and an Expert Advisory Panel [A] [International AI Safety Report 2025](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2025). Expressed as person-hours per individual claim, however, the budget thins rapidly: a single capability indicator may receive only minutes of dedicated reviewer attention because reviewers operate on the document level, not the claim level [C] inferred. Routine outputs (a country surveillance note, a press communique) receive far less.

### D7 — Delegation chain depth

The delegation chain is the deepest of any tier. Tracing an AI-derived claim from a model output through publication can pass: model run -> staff analyst -> drafting team -> internal review -> interdepartmental clearance -> management clearance -> Executive Board or Council -> member-state delegate consultation -> standing working party (e.g., AIGO) -> committee -> ministerial endorsement -> communique drafting -> translation -> final publication. Ten or more discrete handoffs is typical [B] inferred from IMF, OECD and IPCC process documents. Each handoff is *both* a verification opportunity and a context-loss point, because what propagates is the summary, not the underlying reasoning trace.

### D8 — Failure mode profile

Dominant failure modes at this tier are: (i) *committee laundering* — an AI-derived claim becomes a "broadly shared assessment" through repeated paraphrase even though no individual reviewer ever audited it; (ii) *false consensus* — disagreement in the underlying expert pool is collapsed into language acceptable to all delegations, hiding calibration uncertainty; (iii) *frozen evidence* — a claim correct at the time of drafting becomes outdated by the time of publication because revision cycles are slow; (iv) *non-representativeness* — the experts consulted reflect institutional networks rather than the full distribution of relevant expertise, particularly affecting the Global South [B] [Tech Policy Press: Deficits, Gaps, and Compromises in the UN's Governing AI for Humanity](https://www.techpolicy.press/deficits-gaps-and-compromises-in-the-uns-governing-ai-for-humanity/); (v) *citation contamination* — the same hallucination patterns that contaminated NeurIPS 2025 and the Deloitte reports to the Australian and Newfoundland and Labrador governments are likely to occur in international-institution outputs but no published audit has been performed [C] inferred [A] [Fortune: NeurIPS research papers contained 100+ AI-hallucinated citations](https://fortune.com/2026/01/21/neurips-ai-conferences-research-papers-hallucinations/).

### D9 — Substitutes when full verification is infeasible

When full verification is infeasible, S7 substitutes are: (a) *deference to peer institutions* — citing the IPCC, IEA, or another international body as the authoritative source rather than re-verifying the underlying analysis [B] inferred from cross-citation patterns; (b) *delegation of contested claims to country experts* — having member states verify claims about their own jurisdictions; (c) *hedged language* — replacing point estimates with ranges or qualitative descriptors; (d) *footnote and annex offload* — moving the most uncertain claims into background documents that do not require ministerial sign-off; (e) *the standard exchange model* — pointing readers to the AI Standards Exchange Database rather than asserting which standard is authoritative [B] [International AI Standards Exchange](https://aiforgood.itu.int/ai-standards-exchange/).

### D10 — Scale-crossing dynamics

Scale crossing is unusual at S7 because international institutions rarely change tier — but the relevant *cross-tier* dynamic is the propagation of S7 outputs *down* the scale to S6 (sovereign governments) and S4 (large corporations). When the OECD AI Principles cross down into national legislation, the verification chain inverts: a non-binding international assessment that received heavy committee review becomes binding national law that may receive much lighter parliamentary scrutiny [C] inferred from OECD adherence list of 47 jurisdictions [A] [OECD AI Principles Overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles). Conversely, when a large corporation cites an OECD or IMF figure to support a board paper, it is borrowing S7 legitimacy without re-verifying — the entire S5/S4 verification chain collapses to "the OECD said so." The most acute scale-crossing risk is therefore *legitimacy laundering downward*: weak-evidence S7 claims acquiring undeserved bindingness through national legislation or corporate citation.

## 4. Exemplar Cases

### Exemplar 1 — OECD AI Capability Indicators (beta, 2025)

The OECD published in June 2025 a beta framework of nine AI capability indicators developed over five years by 50+ experts, with technical chapters written by 32 experts and peer-reviewed in late 2024 by 25 additional researchers from AI, psychology, and education [A] [OECD AI Capability Indicators](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/introducing-the-oecd-ai-capability-indicators_be745f04-en.html). The framework is intended for use by member-state policymakers in workforce, education and AI-readiness decisions, and an expert survey modeled on the University of Chicago Economic Experts Panel is planned for 2026 to assess unbenchmarked capabilities. The indicators are explicitly published in *beta* form with an invitation for further feedback.

RVCS-1 score for the published indicator framework as a representative deliverable: Q1 source quality 2 (named expert pool, peer-reviewed, but no standardized source-grading scheme); Q2 inferential validity 2 (two-stage expert review and policy-maker feedback, but no adversarial challenge); Q3 normative acceptability 2 (intergovernmental endorsement of the underlying OECD AI Principles framework, but the indicators themselves are not yet endorsed); Q4 confidence calibration 2 (beta status is itself a calibration signal and the OECD acknowledges further standardization is needed); Q5 failure mode awareness 1 (the OECD lists "next steps" but does not provide an AI-specific failure catalog). Total: 9/15.

VTI triple: T_verify / T_total approximately 0.7 (most of the multi-year work is verification rather than novel claim generation); N_reviewers approximately 57 (32 authors + 25 reviewers, with overlap); N_sources high but unenumerated, drawn from a network of AI researchers and an existing PISA-style benchmarking infrastructure. The case illustrates the S7 *high-legitimacy / partial-RVCS* pattern: heavy procedural verification, weak claim-level reasoning verification, explicit beta hedging.

### Exemplar 2 — International AI Safety Report 2025 (UK-commissioned, Bengio-chaired)

Commissioned by the 30 nations attending the 2023 Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, the report assembled 100 experts from 30+ countries plus an Expert Advisory Panel including the EU and UN [A] [International AI Safety Report 2025](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2025). The document is unusual at S7 in that it explicitly catalogues areas of expert disagreement (whether scaling will continue, magnitude of labor impacts, plausibility of loss-of-control scenarios) rather than collapsing them into committee language. The U.S. backed the 2025 report but withheld support for the 2026 update, illustrating the legitimacy-loss sanction mechanism [A] [Time: U.S. Withholds Support](https://time.com/7364551/ai-impact-summit-safety-report/). The report deliberately avoids policy prescriptions and acknowledges that quantitative risk thresholds and robust evidence on the effectiveness of safeguards are largely absent [A] [Computer Weekly: Report highlights disagreement among experts](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366585753/Report-highlights-disagreement-among-experts-on-AI-safety).

RVCS-1 score: Q1 source quality 3 (named expert pool, transparent commissioning, scientific publication track records); Q2 inferential validity 2 (independent secretariat-organized review with civil society and industry input, but no formal adversarial process); Q3 normative acceptability 2 (intergovernmental endorsement, though incomplete after U.S. withdrawal in 2026); Q4 confidence calibration 3 (explicit cataloguing of expert disagreement and acknowledged evaluation gap is a textbook RVCS-1 Q4 implementation); Q5 failure mode awareness 2 (multiple failure modes considered, from misuse to loss of control, but no standardized failure catalog). Total: 12/15.

VTI triple: T_verify / T_total approximately 0.6; N_reviewers >100 (full author and reviewer pool); N_sources very large (the report synthesizes published research). This case illustrates that explicit calibration is *possible* at S7 when the chair has scientific authority and political cover, and demonstrates the highest documented RVCS-1 score for any S7 deliverable identified in this report.

## 5. Counterexamples

The new UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, established by Resolution A/RES/79/325 in August 2025 with 40 members appointed by Secretary-General Guterres in February 2026, is a counterexample-in-design [A] [Independent International Scientific Panel on AI](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en) [B] [UN press: Secretary-General urges trusted scientific guidance](https://press.un.org/en/2026/sgsm23034.doc.htm). It explicitly imports the IPCC review-and-comment process — two-stage expert review, named Review Editors, public comment ledgers — into AI governance and is positioned by the Secretary-General as an "evidence engine" and an "early-warning system" between cutting-edge AI research and policymaking. If the IPCC discipline is reproduced, the panel could push S7 RVCS-1 scores from ~9-12 toward 14-15. The risk is the opposite: the panel may inherit IPCC's calendar slowness against AI's compounding pace, in which case calendar-driven verification (D5) becomes a structural mismatch with the verification problem.

A second partial counterexample is the Oxford AIGI 2025 verification proposal, which would create *technical* verification infrastructure (hardware monitors, mutually verified data centers) for international AI agreements [A] [Verification for International AI Governance](https://aigi.ox.ac.uk/publications/verification-for-international-ai-governance/). This is structurally different from the committee-and-document approach that dominates S7 and would, if adopted, give international institutions an actual technical primitive for compliance verification. Its limitation, for the present framework, is that it explicitly does not address verification of AI-generated *reasoning* used inside the institution — only verification of state compliance with international agreements about AI development.

## 6. Known Failures / Incidents

No published audit has identified a flagship international-institution deliverable that was retracted because of AI-generated reasoning errors as of April 2026. The evidentiary base for S7 failures is therefore *adjacent*, not direct. The most relevant adjacent evidence is: (a) the Deloitte reports to the Australian government (October 2025, A$440,000) and to the Government of Newfoundland and Labrador (November 2025, CA$1.6 million), which contained fabricated academic citations and a fake court quotation, both subsequently corrected [A] [Wikipedia: Hallucination (artificial intelligence)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_(artificial_intelligence)) — these are S6/S5 failures performed *for* governments, indicating that the same drafting practices used by international-institution consultants are vulnerable; (b) the NeurIPS 2025 contamination of accepted papers with hallucinated citations missed by 3+ reviewers per paper, and the parallel ICLR 2026 finding [A] [Fortune coverage](https://fortune.com/2026/01/21/neurips-ai-conferences-research-papers-hallucinations/) — international institutions cite peer-reviewed AI literature heavily and inherit any contamination upstream; (c) the U.S. withdrawal of support from the 2026 International AI Safety Report, which is not a reasoning-verification failure per se but is a legitimacy-loss event of the kind D4 predicts. The absence of documented S7 retractions could reflect either genuine effectiveness of committee verification *or* absence of audit. Given the OECD's own beta hedging on its capability indicators, the second explanation should not be ruled out [C] inferred.

## 7. Open Questions

1. Has any international institution documented an internal reasoning-verification protocol specifically for AI-generated drafting input, beyond generic editorial review?
2. What is the actual per-claim reviewer attention budget for an OECD or IMF deliverable that integrates AI-derived analysis — measured in minutes rather than total person-years?
3. Will the UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI adopt the IPCC public comment ledger, including publication of all reviewer comments and author responses, and if so, will it cover AI-generated reasoning steps or only the underlying scientific claims?
4. How is the failure mode of *legitimacy laundering downward* (D10) actually behaving in practice — when a national legislature cites OECD AI guidance, does any verification re-occur or does the citation pass through unaudited?
5. Are S7 deliverables vulnerable to the same hallucinated-citation contamination found in NeurIPS 2025, ICLR 2026, and the Deloitte government reports, and what would a systematic audit look like?
