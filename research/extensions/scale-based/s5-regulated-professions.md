# Tier S5 — Regulated Professions and Legal Institutions

status: research_in_progress
tier: S5
date: 2026-04-13

## 1. Tier Definition and Scope

Tier S5 covers organizations whose practitioners hold a state-issued professional license under an enforceable code of ethics, where personal liability cannot be discharged onto an employer or a software vendor. The four sub-tiers used here: **legal services** (solo through Big Law / Magic Circle), **medical practice and clinical AI users** (independent physicians through academic medical centers), **public accountancy and audit** (solo CPAs through the Big Four), and **licensed financial advice** (solo RIAs through bulge-bracket wealth divisions).

The defining structural property of S5 is that **verification of AI-generated reasoning is a professional-liability problem bound to a code of ethics**, not merely a commercial-prudence problem (S1–S4) and not a sovereignty or rule-making problem (S6–S7). When an S5 practitioner accepts a fabricated AI output and acts on it, three accountability surfaces are touched at once: civil malpractice, professional discipline (bar, medical board, PCAOB, SEC Division of Examinations), and court or regulator sanction (Rule 11, FDA enforcement on clinical software, PCAOB inspection). The triple-surface property makes S5 the most data-rich tier in the 7-tier scale: every AI failure that reaches a hearing produces a public, indexed, primary-source artifact. For the legal sub-tier, the documented incident base exceeds 1,170 court decisions noting AI-generated hallucinations as of April 2026 — more empirical material on AI-reasoning-failure-in-deployment than any other sector currently offers. Grade: **A**.

The S5 ↔ S4 boundary is **personal license, not headcount**: a 50-person law firm is S5; a 50-person SaaS company is S4. The S5 ↔ S6 boundary is whether the institution **issues** the rules (S6) or **operates under** them (S5).

## 2. Core Question Answered

**Question**: Do existing professional ethics rules actually define a reasoning verification method, or do they delegate it to individual judgment, and how does the answer differ between large and small firms within the same profession?

**Answer**: Across all four S5 sub-tiers, the formal ethics rules **assert the duty** of verification but **delegate the method** entirely to the practitioner. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024), the FDA GMLP guiding principles (October 2021, refreshed January 2025 by IMDRF), AICPA SAS 145 (effective December 2023), and the SEC's 2025 / 2026 Examination Priorities for investment advisers all converge on the same structural pattern: each requires that humans understand the limitations of the AI tool, supervise its use, and remain responsible for the output, but **none specifies a step-by-step verification procedure, a minimum source-quality bar, a review-layer count, or a scoring rubric**. The codes are duty-based, not protocol-based. The practical consequence is that within the same profession, large firms have substituted **internal SOPs, cite-checking layers, and proprietary RAG-grounded tools** for the absent regulatory protocol, while solo and small firms have only the practitioner's own habits — which the empirical incident record shows are insufficient. The within-tier variation is therefore not a difference in *rules* but a difference in *infrastructure substituted for missing rules*. Grade: **A** (Opinion 512 text, FDA GMLP, SAS 145, and SEC Exam Priorities documents are all primary).

Three downstream implications structure the rest of this report. (1) **Failure mode prediction**: where the rule delegates the method, misgrounding (real sources cited for propositions they do not contain) dominates fabrication (sources that do not exist), because misgrounding survives a casual personal review while fabrication is caught by the first cite-check. Grade: **B**. (2) **Sanction calibration**: courts must reverse-engineer the protocol post hoc; 2025–2026 sanction opinions read more like incremental rule-making than routine discipline. Grade: **B**. (3) **Within-tier divergence**: the absence of a prescribed protocol means firms with capital can outspend firms without. S5 is the cleanest natural experiment for the proposition that **verification infrastructure is purchasable, but verification capability is not transferable**. Grade: **C**.

## 3. Comparative Dimensions (D1–D10)

Each dimension scored 0–3 with grade in brackets.

- **D1 — Decision stakes per AI-mediated decision**: 3/3. A single misgrounded citation in securities work can trigger seven-figure malpractice exposure plus bar referral; a hallucinated diagnosis can produce patient injury; a misgrounded audit memo can trigger PCAOB enforcement. **A**.
- **D2 — Decision velocity**: 2/3. Litigation associates draft 10–40 motions or memos per week; physicians see 60–120 patients; CPAs touch dozens of working papers per audit week. **C** for the specific numbers.
- **D3 — Reversibility of error**: 1/3. Court filings can be amended but the docket entry is permanent; medical errors are partially reversible only if caught quickly; audit opinions become public market record once issued. **A**.
- **D4 — Verification cost relative to total decision cost**: 0–3/3, highly variable. Big Law cite-checking and partner review consume an estimated 15–30% of associate time on a brief; in a solo practice, verification is whatever time remains after substantive work, often <5%. The verification-cost ratio is an inverse function of firm size. **B**.
- **D5 — Number of formal review layers between AI output and decision**: large firms 2–4 layers (associate self-review, senior associate review, partner review, optional cite-check team), solo practice 0–1 layers. **A** for Big Law, **B** for solo (inferred from sanction case fact patterns).
- **D6 — Penalty for unverified AI output**: explicit and steeply increasing. ABA Model Rule 3.3 and Federal Rule 11 provide direct sanctions authority; bar referral can lead to suspension or disbarment; FDA can issue Warning Letters; PCAOB can issue Part II enforcement; SEC can bring AI-washing and fiduciary-breach actions. The densest penalty surface in any tier. **A**.
- **D7 — Existence of a regulator-issued written verification protocol**: 1/3 existence, 0/3 specificity. Opinion 512 says lawyers must "verify accuracy" but does not say what verification means operationally. FDA GMLP describes good-practice principles with no clinician-facing rubric. SAS 145 requires considering IT-related risks but does not script AI-output review. SEC tells advisers to "incorporate human reviewers" without defining the protocol. **A**.
- **D8 — Existence of a within-firm written verification SOP**: heavily bimodal. Big Law has firm-level SOPs (DLA Piper's "audit the robots" framework, Morgan & Morgan's post-incident mandatory training, internal Harvey/CoCounsel deployment SOPs at AmLaw 100 firms); solo and small firms typically have none. The bimodal distribution is the central finding of this tier. **B**.
- **D9 — Decision-maker AI fluency**: ~1.5/3 average with extreme variance. Clio 2025 and Smokeball 2025 show 53% of small-firm lawyers using GenAI but only ~40% using legal-specific tools (down from 58% in 2024 as free-tier general AI became default); firms of 51+ lawyers use legal-specific tools at roughly double the small-firm rate. Fluency predicts adoption rate, not verification rigor. **B**.
- **D10 — Public visibility of AI failures**: 3/3 — highest of any tier. Court orders, bar discipline, FDA Warning Letters, and SEC enforcement are all public; the Charlotin AI Hallucination Cases Database and Volokh / Reason tracker make the legal corpus searchable. **A**.

**Composite**: S5 combines the highest stakes, densest regulation, and most public failures with the **least operationally specific** verification protocols of any regulated environment in the study. The gap between rule density and protocol specificity is the diagnostic feature.

## 4. Exemplar Cases

### Exemplar 1 — Wadsworth v. Walmart (D. Wyo. 2025), Morgan & Morgan motion in limine

Three Morgan & Morgan PA attorneys (AmLaw 100, ~1,000+ lawyers) used the firm's in-house AI platform to draft a motion in limine in a federal products liability case. The platform generated eight non-existent citations; the brief was filed without manual cite-checking. The court issued an Order to Show Cause and sanctioned the drafter $3,000 plus pro hac vice revocation, with $1,000 each against two supervising attorneys. Grade: **A** (Bloomberg Law, ABA Journal, LawSites primary reporting).

**RVCS-1 of the AI-generated reasoning chain**: Q1 source quality **0** (fabricated, Level 1); Q2 inferential validity **1** (legal arguments structurally plausible); Q3 normative acceptability **0** (Rule 3.3 and Rule 11 violations); Q4 confidence calibration **0** (no uncertainty markers); Q5 failure mode awareness **0** (drafter unaware of fabrication risk in the in-house tool). **Total: 1/15**. **VTI**: T_verify/T_total ≈ 0; N_reviewers ≈ 1.5; N_sources ≈ 0.

### Exemplar 2 — AmLaw 50 firm M&A diligence memo workflow using Harvey

Representative composite based on Bloomberg Law and Artificial Lawyer reporting on Big Law Harvey deployments. The standard internal SOP requires (a) Harvey output review by the drafting associate, (b) cite-check by a junior associate or paralegal against Westlaw or Lexis, (c) senior-associate review of legal conclusions, and (d) partner sign-off before client delivery. Grade: **B** (the broad shape is reported, the exact step counts vary by firm and matter).

**RVCS-1 of the post-SOP output the partner signs off on**: Q1 **3** (sources checked against primary law); Q2 **2** (senior associate flags novel inferences); Q3 **2** (four-layer review surfaces ethics conflicts); Q4 **2** (partner sign-off forces a "bet my license" judgment); Q5 **2** (post-Mata internal training on AI failure modes). **Total: 11/15**. **VTI**: T_verify/T_total ≈ 0.20–0.30 (Grade **C** for the precise number); N_reviewers ≈ 3–4; N_sources ≈ 1 primary source per cited proposition.

### Exemplar 3 — Solo immigration practitioner federal-court motion

Composite consistent with multiple 2025–2026 cases (Massachusetts $2,000, New Orleans $1,000, California $10,000, the Brigandi $110,000 outlier). Solo practitioner with no associate or paralegal uses ChatGPT free tier to draft a motion responding to a USCIS denial; the motion is filed without cite-checking; four hallucinated citations are caught by the court. Modal sanction $2,000–$5,000 plus state-bar referral. Grade: **A** for the sanction-pattern shape, **B** for the composite construction.

**RVCS-1**: Q1 **0**; Q2 **1**; Q3 **0**; Q4 **0**; Q5 **0**. **Total: 1/15**. **VTI**: T_verify/T_total = 0; N_reviewers = 1; N_sources = 0.

The Exemplar 1, 2, 3 totals (1, 11, 1) are the central comparative finding: **the same class of model output scores 1/15 in a zero-review setting and 11/15 in a four-review setting, even when the underlying model is identical**. Verification infrastructure substitutes for the missing regulatory protocol.

## 5. Counterexamples

**Counterexample A — Big Law also fails**. Morgan & Morgan is the 42nd-largest U.S. law firm yet its in-house AI platform produced eight fabricated citations that reached a federal docket; Boies Schiller Flexner and Gordon Rees have also appeared in the 2025–2026 sanction record. The naive "more reviewers = no failures" inference is wrong; **a flawed SOP can be worse than no SOP** because it creates false confidence that displaces individual verification responsibility. Grade: **A**.

**Counterexample B — Some solo practitioners verify carefully**. A non-trivial subset of solo practitioners (often former Big Law associates) carry the cite-check discipline into solo practice and do not appear in sanction databases. Absence of sanction is not proof of verification quality, but is consistent with a "verification capability travels with the practitioner, not with the firm" reading. Grade: **C**.

Together, the right model of S5 verification is **firm SOP × practitioner habit**, not firm size alone.

## 6. Known Failures / Incidents

### The 1,174-case corpus

As of April 2026, the AI Hallucination Cases Database maintained by Damien Charlotin (HEC Paris Smart Law Hub) has indexed **more than 1,170 court and tribunal decisions worldwide** in which judges have addressed AI-generated content (fabricated citations, hallucinated quotations, misattributed authority, misgrounded source claims) in more than passing reference. The database stood at roughly 719 incidents in January 2026 and crossed 1,170 by mid-April 2026 — a net addition of 450+ cases in roughly 100 days, or **four to six new cases per court working day**. Q1 2026 alone produced approximately $145,000 in U.S. monetary sanctions specifically attributable to AI-fabricated content. Grade: **A** (Charlotin database is a primary aggregator; Reason / Volokh corroborates the velocity; Sterne Kessler 2025 year-in-review confirms the year-on-year escalation).

The 1,174 figure is significant for three reasons. First, it is a **lower bound**: the database only counts decisions where the court addressed AI use in more than passing reference, so the true incidence is higher. Second, the corpus is distributed **across the full firm-size spectrum** — solo practitioners, mid-size firms, and AmLaw 100 firms all appear, with sanctioned attorneys including those at 1,000+ lawyer firms with dedicated AI infrastructure. Third, the corpus has begun to **stratify by failure type**: solo and small-firm cases predominantly involve full fabrication, while larger-firm cases more often involve misgrounding (real cases cited for propositions they do not contain) or quote-misattribution. The misgrounding pattern is more professionally dangerous because it survives cursory review — a junior associate cite-checking by case name will find the case, not realize it does not say what the brief claims, and pass it forward. The 1,174-case corpus is therefore not just a count of failures but a longitudinal natural experiment on what verification habits catch what AI errors. Grade: **B**.

## 7. Within-Tier Variation: Large Firm vs Small Firm

This is the central analytical contribution of the S5 report. Within-tier variation in S5 is larger than in any other tier in the 7-tier scale, because the same professional rules govern firms of dramatically different infrastructure capacities.

### The structural asymmetry

Big Law (broadly the AmLaw 100, ~250+ lawyers) operates AI-generated reasoning through what amounts to a **manufactured human review pipeline** with four features:

1. **A deployment SOP** — written internal policy on which AI tools associates may use, on which matters, with what mandatory verification steps. DLA Piper's "audit the robots" framework (Bloomberg Law) is the public-facing version of an SOP most AmLaw 100 firms have adopted in some form since the 2023 Mata sanctions. Grade: **B**.
2. **A grounded-tool stack** — Harvey, CoCounsel, and other RAG-grounded products that retrieve from Westlaw, Lexis, or Practical Law. Magesh et al. (*JELS* 2025) documents that even these grounded tools hallucinate at 17–33%, but this is dramatically lower than the 58–82% rate for general-purpose ChatGPT and is the single most important infrastructure substitution Big Law has made. Grade: **A**.
3. **A cite-check layer** — junior associates or specialized paralegals verifying authorities against primary sources. The role pre-existed AI as a litigation QC step; its post-AI repurposing is a low-cost retrofit.
4. **A partner-sign-off bottleneck** — the matter partner's signature is the legal moment at which professional liability attaches; the signature ritual is a forcing function for the partner to ask "would I bet my license on this." Big Law has not changed this workflow with AI; it has *preserved* it as the load-bearing safeguard.

Together the four features substitute for the absent regulator-issued protocol. The substitute is imperfect (Morgan & Morgan, Boies Schiller, and other AmLaw 100 firms have still appeared in sanction filings) but it materially improves verification. The Section 4 exemplars show the magnitude: the same model output scores 1/15 in a no-review setting and 11/15 in a four-review setting on RVCS-1.

Solo and small-firm practitioners (broadly: <10 lawyer firms) operate without any of the four features. (1) **No deployment SOP**: Clio 2025 and Smokeball 2025 show small-firm AI policy adoption lags adoption of AI tools themselves by 12–24 months. (2) **No grounded-tool stack**: small firms disproportionately use general-purpose AI (free ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) rather than legal-specific RAG tools; only ~40% of small-firm legal professionals use legal-specific AI, *down* from 58% in 2024 as free-tier general AI tools became the default. The shift toward general-purpose tools is itself a verification-quality regression because general tools hallucinate at 2–3× the rate of grounded legal tools. Grade: **A**. (3) **No cite-check layer**: by definition, the solo practitioner has no junior to delegate to. (4) **No partner-sign-off bottleneck**: the solo practitioner is the partner; the sign-off is self-administered and the forcing function dissolves.

### Adoption-rate paradox

**Small-firm AI adoption is rising fast and Big Law adoption is rising more slowly**. Smokeball 2025 shows small-firm GenAI integration at 53%, up from 27% in 2023; Clio 2025 is in the same range. AmLaw 100 firms are at 70–87% depending on the survey, but their growth rate is slower because they were earlier adopters. Grade: **A**. The paradox: the population most exposed to fabrication-class failures (small firms using general-purpose AI without verification infrastructure) is also growing fastest in adoption, producing the empirically observed pattern that the absolute number of small-firm sanction cases is rising disproportionately fast even as large firms also appear in the record.

### Cross-sub-tier replication

The legal pattern replicates with sub-tier shading in the other three S5 sub-tiers.

**Medicine**: academic medical centers have IRB review, clinical informatics committees, EHR-integration governance, and (for FDA-cleared devices) an explicit submission paper trail; solo and small-clinic physicians have none of these. The 2025 Nature *npj Digital Medicine* study found 93.3% of FDA-cleared medical devices reported no information on training data source — the **absence** of substantive verification documentation even in the regulator-approved tier is the closest medical analog to the legal "rules-without-protocol" pattern. Grade: **A**. **Accounting**: the Big Four (Deloitte's Zora AI, KPMG's Workbench, EY tax tooling, PwC's 315,000-employee training spend) operate proprietary AI agents inside quality-management infrastructure tied to PCAOB inspection. SAS 145 imposes risk-assessment duties but does not script AI-output review. Mid-tier and small CPAs use general-purpose AI without firm-level controls. Grade: **B**. **Investment advice**: large RIAs and bulge-bracket wealth divisions operate compliance functions the SEC's 2025 / 2026 Exam Priorities have indicated will be tested for AI-related controls; solo RIAs operate under the same fiduciary duty rule with no compliance function. Morrison Foerster (October 2025) and Venable (December 2025) practitioner guidance is consistent. Grade: **A**.

### Section 7 finding

**The within-tier S5 variation is not best understood as a difference in "rules" but as a difference in "infrastructure substituted for missing rules."** ABA ethics rules, FDA GMLP, AICPA SAS 145, and SEC fiduciary expectations are uniform within each profession. What varies is whether the practitioner has access to a deployment SOP, a grounded-tool stack, a cite-check layer, and a partner-sign-off bottleneck. Large firms purchased the four features; solo practitioners have not. Where the rules delegate the method to individual judgment, verification capacity becomes a function of individual habit plus institutional substitute — and where the institutional substitute is absent, individual habit alone has demonstrably been insufficient against the modal AI failure modes. This is the strongest empirical case in the 7-tier scale for the proposition that **regulation that asserts duty without specifying method produces a verification quality that scales with firm capital, not with rule strength**. Grade: **B**.

## 8. Open Questions

1. **How much of the within-tier variation collapses if formal ethics rules add an operational protocol?** ABA, several state bars, and the FDA are all considering more operationally specific guidance. If a 2026–2027 update to Opinion 512 specifies a verification protocol (mandatory cite-check against a primary-source database, mandatory source-quality documentation for each cited authority), firm-size variation should shrink. S5 offers a near-term test of whether protocol-level rules can substitute for infrastructure-level capital.
2. **Are misgrounding-class failures rising faster than fabrication-class failures?** Section 6 stratification is qualitative; a quantitative coding of the 1,174-case corpus is needed. If misgrounding is rising faster, ethics-rule updates should focus on source-quality assessment rather than existence-of-citation checks.
3. **Does the cross-sub-tier pattern hold for medicine and finance with the same magnitude as for law?** The legal corpus is the cleanest record because court orders are public; medical malpractice is partly sealed by settlement and financial enforcement is fragmented across SEC, FINRA, and state regulators. A coordinated cross-regulator data release would be needed to test the analogy.
4. **Is professional-liability insurance pricing yet reflecting the AI-verification gap?** If insurers price AI-related malpractice into solo and small-firm premiums, market-based risk pricing might create a verification-infrastructure subsidy (insurers requiring cite-check tooling as a coverage condition). As of April 2026, insurer behavior is still emerging.
5. **What role does "verification capability that travels with the practitioner" play?** If the variable is *practitioner training history* rather than *current firm size*, the within-tier story changes meaningfully.
6. **How does the 17% RAG hallucination rate interact with the cite-check workflow?** A 17% rate means even the best Big Law tool produces a misgrounded or fabricated output once every six queries. If a cite-checker assumes RAG output is trustworthy (level inflation), each layer relies on the previous layer and the pipeline-trust architecture becomes a verification target itself.
