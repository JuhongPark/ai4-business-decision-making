## 1. Method

Evidence was gathered through targeted web search over 2024–2026 engineering and journalism sources: vendor engineering blogs (Intercom, Harvey, Shopify, Replit, Duolingo, Stripe, Morgan Stanley, Sourcegraph), customer case studies hosted by OpenAI and Anthropic, venture-firm analyses (Bessemer, First Round, Sequoia), business press (Fortune, CNBC, VentureBeat, TechCrunch, American Banker), developer community threads (Cursor forum, GitHub community discussions), and aggregated LLMOps case-study indices (ZenML LLMOps Database, Pragmatic Engineer). Search queries combined vendor names with "model switch", "migration", "engineering months", "validation", and "eval" to surface primary and secondary reports. Where engineering-month switching cost was not explicitly stated in source text, ranges were inferred from reported calendar time ("in a few days", "four months", "18 months of A/B tests") and typical team sizes for the named artifact, and the inference is labelled as such. Primary evidence window is 2022–2026; one 2022 anchor (pre-GPT-4 Intercom) is included to establish the baseline. Negative evidence — cases searched for but not located — is recorded in Section 5.

## 3. Pattern findings

**Pattern 1 — "Eval harness is the moat, not the model" [high confidence]**
Observed: Intercom ran 120+ A/B tests against a production baseline over 18 months; Harvey publishes BigLaw Bench with lawyer-designed rubrics on three pillars; Shopify routes all AI traffic through an LLM proxy; JPMorgan updates its LLM Suite on an 8-week cycle; Stripe iterated on label data before model upgrades. Inference: organizations that built a reusable evaluation/abstraction layer before the first model swap paid switching cost once and amortize it across all subsequent transitions. Those without the harness pay the cost every time.

**Pattern 2 — "Decision authority migrates from CTO to product + risk as the system hits production scale" [medium-high confidence]**
Observed: Early-stage decisions (Intercom Fin 1, Replit Ghostwriter) sit with engineering / ML leadership; at scale and in regulated domains, decision authority broadens — Klarna's CEO owned the public positioning and walk-back, JPMorgan's Chief Data & Analytics Officer + compliance committees gate every provider, Morgan Stanley routes through CIO + Compliance & Risk, Harvey exposes model selection to the customer (authority pushed out one level). Inference: authority structure is a function of application criticality × regulatory posture, not company size alone.

**Pattern 3 — "The dominant failure mode is economic regression, not capability regression" [medium confidence]**
Observed: Klarna had to re-introduce human agents for complex cases and quality-managed its walk-back; Duolingo's Max tier dropped gross margin ~2 percentage points attributable to GenAI compute; GitHub Copilot community reported silent fallback (Claude 3.7 → GPT-4o) — a cost-driven routing behavior that users experienced as a capability failure. Inference: when model swaps fail, they fail on unit economics or silent downgrade far more visibly than on raw task accuracy. Primary evidence window shows a shift from "does it work" (2023) to "does it work at this cost" (2025+).

**Pattern 4 — "Switching cost is bimodal — cheap on a gateway, expensive without one" [high confidence]**
Observed cheap swaps (days to weeks): Intercom Fin on Claude live at Anthropic "in under a week"; Intercom GPT-4 → GPT-4.1 "in a few days"; Sourcegraph Cody default changes; Cursor default changes; Shopify proxy-mediated swaps. Observed expensive swaps or rebuilds (quarters): Klarna initial build (USD 2–3M); Replit v1 → v2 Agent rework; Morgan Stanley firm-wide deployment (98% adoption implies multi-quarter); JPMorgan LLM Suite build. Inference: once a model gateway / eval harness / abstraction layer exists, marginal switching cost collapses toward zero; the real question is whether the gateway itself is built.

**Pattern 5 — "Task-specialization is replacing single-model defaults" [high confidence]**
Observed: Harvey explicitly states "no longer a single best model, but an array of premier models, each particularly suited to different legal tasks"; Perplexity offers Sonar/GPT/Claude/Gemini by mode; Glean sees "specialization of models with Claude for coding, GPT for reasoning"; Cursor routes Opus 4.5 → Sonnet 4.5 by usage tier; Hebbia's financial-services benchmark shows Claude 4 Opus best at extraction, o3/GPT-5 best at strategic analysis. Inference: the 2023 question "which LLM should we use?" has been replaced by the 2025–2026 question "how do we route by task?" — the abstraction unit has shifted from model to task-model pairing.

**Pattern 6 — "Regulated and high-criticality systems wait one generation behind frontier" [medium confidence]**
Observed: Morgan Stanley ran through 2024 on GPT-4 family without public GPT-4o migration; JPMorgan's LLM Suite updates on an 8-week cycle rather than on release date; FDA and FINRA guidance still maturing through 2025–2026. Inference: regulated and critical-path applications trade ~3–6 months of capability lag for validation, governance, and rollback safety — a rational tax on criticality × regulatory posture. Weak evidence for exact lag number — this is a directional claim, not a measured one.

## 4. Decision framework — when to swap vs when to stay

The decision is a function of three axes: **application criticality** (low / medium / high / life-critical), **regulatory posture** (permissive / compliance-relevant / regulated / FDA-HIPAA-FINRA class), and **capability gap** (capability-equivalent / incremental / generational / categorically new).

### 4.1 Decision tree (nested bullets)

- **(Q1) Does a gateway / eval harness / abstraction layer exist?**
  - NO — before any swap, build the harness first. Do not attempt to swap under production pressure without one. Expected build: 2–6 months at mid-size; it pays for itself by the second swap. (Shopify, Harvey, Intercom, JPMorgan evidence.)
  - YES — proceed to Q2.
- **(Q2) What is the capability gap on the eval harness?**
  - Capability-equivalent (≤5% delta on primary metrics) — **STAY**. Switching cost (even on a gateway) is non-zero; no expected return. (Stripe evidence: data quality dominated model choice.)
  - Incremental (5–20% delta, same task family) — **STAY unless cost-driven**. If the new model is materially cheaper at equivalent quality, swap on the gateway; if not, the organizational churn is not worth the capability delta.
  - Generational (20–50% delta, or enables a new sub-task) — **SWAP**, conditional on Q3.
  - Categorically new (enables a task that was previously infeasible — e.g., agent loops, long context, vision) — **SWAP aggressively**, even at higher integration cost; competitive exposure of not swapping exceeds cost of swapping.
- **(Q3) What is the application criticality × regulatory posture?**
  - Low criticality + permissive — ship to production with light canary; rollback path required.
  - Medium criticality + compliance-relevant — shadow-deploy alongside incumbent, compare on full eval harness, graduate by traffic ramp (1% → 10% → 50% → 100%) with at least one full business cycle at each step.
  - High criticality + regulated — require (a) full eval-harness pass, (b) explicit risk/compliance sign-off, (c) rollback ready on minutes-scale, (d) a documented failure mode library. Plan for ~1 generation of lag versus frontier.
  - Life-critical or FDA/HIPAA/FINRA class — bind model version; change is a regulated event, not an engineering event. Swap only when the new capability is credibility-plan-ready per FDA-style frameworks. Expect 6–12 months of lag.
- **(Q4) What is the fallback design?**
  - Always swap *in addition to*, not *instead of*, the incumbent for the first full business cycle. Silent fallback (Copilot 3.7 → GPT-4o case) is a failure mode that must be instrumented and alerted.
- **(Q5) Authority and sign-off at swap time.**
  - Default authority = product / ML lead if criticality ≤ medium and regulation ≤ compliance-relevant.
  - Authority escalates to CTO + Risk/Compliance lead at high criticality.
  - Authority escalates to CEO/board framing at enterprise-wide, externally-visible swaps (Klarna, Intercom Fin 2, Shopify mandate) because the swap is a strategic communication event.

## 5. Open questions and negative evidence

**Unresolved questions**
- Exact eng-month switching cost for the dominant cases is under-reported. Most sources give calendar time or dollar investment; deriving an eng-month number requires assuming team size and is inference, not observation.
- Quantitative failure-rate data (e.g., what fraction of model swaps produce user-visible regression within 30 days) is not publicly available at portfolio level. Existing post-mortems are self-selected to the "survived" side.
- Regulated-industry lag (Pattern 6) is asserted directionally; I did not find a published data series plotting frontier-release to regulated-production lag across domains.
- Decision-authority evolution over the lifetime of a single system (e.g., how Fin 1's authority structure differed from Fin 2's) is under-documented.

**Negative evidence — searched but not located**
- Specific engineering-month switching-cost estimates from Shopify, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan LLM Suite (all report scale or dollars but not eng-months).
- A published Instacart model-migration case study for 2024 (searched; not found).
- A published Superhuman or Descript model-migration case (Superhuman publicly remains an OpenAI shop; migration absent).
- LinkedIn-specific LLM model-switch post-mortem (tech-stack evolution post found; switch post not).
- Any public failure post-mortem of Klarna's AI-first-to-hybrid walk-back with engineering detail (press coverage only; no technical post-mortem).
- Detailed regulated-industry swap case studies with FDA/HIPAA-specific validation documentation in primary sources (search returned framework-level articles, not case-level ones).
- Quantified "silent fallback" incidence beyond the GitHub Copilot community thread.

## 6. Feedback to the three-pillar adaptability frame

- **Pillar 1 (AI usage) — evaluation is the load-bearing adaptability layer, not the model.** The strongest firms (Intercom, Harvey, Shopify, JPMorgan) adapt by investing in the harness above the model. This shifts the A1 question from "which model do we use?" to "do we have the harness that lets model choice be cheap?" Adaptability at the AI-usage pillar is equivalent to harness maturity.
- **Pillar 1 → Pillar 2 (organization) — decision authority is itself a dynamic variable.** Authority moves from ML/CTO at early-stage to product + risk + CEO at scale and under regulation. An adaptable organization has an explicit authority-escalation ladder for model swaps, not a fixed owner. This feeds O2 (role redefinition cycles) directly.
- **Pillar 3 (technical infrastructure) — the gateway/proxy is the adaptability substrate.** Shopify's LibreChat fork, JPMorgan's LLM Suite, Perplexity's Sonar-plus-router, and Cursor's model menu are the same architectural pattern in different domains: the abstraction layer is the unit of infrastructure investment that decouples model turnover from product turnover. This directly feeds T1 (build vs rent) and T3 (technical debt — prompts-as-code live above this layer).
- **Cross-pillar — the dominant failure mode in 2024–2026 is economic, not capability.** Klarna's walk-back, Duolingo's margin compression, and Copilot's silent fallback are all cost-driven failures that surfaced as quality regressions to users. An adaptability frame that tracks only capability deltas will miss the dominant 2025–2026 failure mode. M2 (measurable indicators) should include at least one unit-economics-regression-after-swap metric.
- **Regulated-domain branch — adaptability does not mean synchrony with the frontier.** Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, and the FDA-guidance-bound healthcare domain (cross-reference: healthcare-bio-ai track) adapt by buying staged lag rather than by swapping first. The adaptability frame should carry "intentional lag tier" as a first-class design choice, not a deficit to be corrected.
- **For M1 (systems-dynamics map) — there is a reinforcing loop between harness maturity and swap speed.** Each swap that flows through the harness both exercises and strengthens it, which lowers the cost of the next swap, which increases willingness to swap, which exercises the harness further. The delay is the harness build itself — a stock that, once full, turns subsequent flows cheap. This is a candidate named loop for M1 ("evaluation flywheel") with an identifiable leverage point at the moment the harness is first funded.
